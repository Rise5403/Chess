How to play

Everything the game enforces, including the three rules people usually have to look up.

How the pieces move

PieceMovement
PawnOne square forward, or two from its starting rank. Captures one square diagonally forward — the only piece that captures differently from how it moves.
KnightTwo squares one way and one square the other. The only piece that can jump over others.
BishopAny distance diagonally. It stays on its starting colour for the whole game.
RookAny distance along a rank or file.
QueenAny distance in any of the eight directions.
KingOne square in any direction, and it may never move onto an attacked square.

The three special rules

Castling

The king moves two squares toward a rook and that rook jumps to the far side of it. It is the only move where two pieces move at once. All of the following must be true:

  • Neither the king nor that rook has moved at any point in the game.
  • Every square between them is empty.
  • The king is not in check now, does not pass through an attacked square, and does not land on one.

Passing through an attacked square is the condition most often missed. Select your king and the game will only offer castling when all of it holds.

En passant

When a pawn advances two squares and lands beside an enemy pawn, that pawn may capture it as though it had only moved one square. The offer expires immediately — if you do not take it on your very next move, it is gone.

Promotion

A pawn reaching the far rank must become a queen, rook, bishop or knight. It cannot stay a pawn and it cannot become a king. Nearly always a queen, but a knight is occasionally stronger because it reaches squares a queen cannot.

How a game ends

Checkmate

The king is attacked and no legal move removes the attack. The attacking side wins. The king is never actually captured.

Stalemate

The side to move has no legal move but is not in check. The game is drawn. This is the rule that rescues losing positions, and the reason a lone king is not a formality.

The three drawing rules

  • Threefold repetition — the same position, with the same side to move and the same castling and en passant options, occurs three times.
  • Fifty-move rule — fifty moves by each side pass with no capture and no pawn move.
  • Insufficient material — neither side has the material to force mate: king against king, king and one knight, king and one bishop, or two bishops on the same colour.

Reading the notation

The move list uses standard algebraic notation, the same as any chess book.

WrittenMeans
e4A pawn moves to e4. Pawns have no letter.
Nf3A knight moves to f3. N is used because K is the king.
exd5The pawn on the e-file captures on d5.
Nbd2Two knights could reach d2; the one on the b-file went.
